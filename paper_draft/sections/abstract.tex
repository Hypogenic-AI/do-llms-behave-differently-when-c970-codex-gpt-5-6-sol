Synthetic evaluations and agent-to-agent systems often use model-written prompts, even though deployment traffic includes human-written requests. If a responding model recognizes synthetic style and changes its behavior, evaluation estimates may not transfer. We tested this possibility with 120 items spanning harmful compliance, benign over-refusal, sycophancy, and multiple-choice accuracy. For each item, we constructed content-matched human-style and LLM-style variants, validated them with a blinded judge, and queried two API models. We complemented this paired audit with surface-feature adjustment, original-prompt and explicit-source-label controls, and a white-box probe and steering experiment on Qwen3-8B. The manipulation succeeded: LLM-style variants received 13.1--41.9 percentage points higher judged AI-authorship probability across tasks. However, none of six preregistered primary model--task contrasts was significant after Holm correction, and no contrast met our corrected-significance and greater-than-10-point criterion. Qwen3-8B encoded authorship style strongly: a held-out linear probe reached 0.907 AUROC, compared with 0.601 for a surface-only baseline. Yet adding or subtracting one activation standard deviation along the probe direction changed aggregate behavior by the same +6.25 points, a shift matched by a norm-matched random direction. These results identify a recognition--behavior dissociation: prompt style was both perceived and linearly decodable, but this pilot provides no evidence that the decoded feature causally selects the measured behaviors. The finding is a bounded null, not an equivalence claim.
